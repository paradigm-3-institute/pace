Crucial decisions will soon be made about the pace of frontier artificial intelligence — that is, the rate at which frontier AI capabilities are developed, deployed, and diffused. How should companies balance the capability of their models against the effectiveness of their oversight? How should regulators handle the spread of systems that can launch advanced cyber attacks? How should governments navigate arms-race dynamics, in which each side fears that its own restraint will be exploited?

Many of these decisions will need to be made quickly, without all the relevant information, and balancing competing interests. And these initial choices will substantially shape the precedents, institutional knowledge, and evidence that will guide future decisions.

In fact pacing [is](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/jun/13/anthropic-disable-advanced-ai-models-us-government-order) [here](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/07/openai-astra-model-delay-cybersecurity-risks) [already](https://ai-frontiers.org/articles/us-chip-export-controls-china-ai); the question is whether we go on to do it well. That is, whether we end up actually reducing the risks, including the risks induced by pacing. We already have targeted safety commitments, self-regulatory lab policies, and [active](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/8/6/what-came-into-force-with-the-eus-ai-act-this-week-and-what-didnt) AI legislation. The industry is also governed by broader regulations, like those covering the export of [advanced chips](https://muse.jhu.edu/pub/1/oa_edited_volume/chapter/4174906), [intellectual property rights](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781003725756-12/technology-fire-bhanuprabha-brahma-bithika-saikia-daniel-balsalobre-lorente-rujab-muchahary), and [laws on immigration](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3461702.3462617). As AI progress grows even more consequential and complex, it is vital that these interventions are well-designed, proportionate, and workable for competing states and companies.

The AI pacing question presently involves a range of unstated assumptions. For instance, informal discussion often implicitly assumes a monolithic “AI progress speed”: one knob that can be dialed up and down. But there is no such dial. The actual range of interventions available to us is instead broad and high-dimensional, with intense path dependencies (e.g. will the prerequisite industries for frontier progress still exist after a frontier slowdown?). There are many ways to intervene, many moments to do so, and many different underlying goals which might motivate an intervention.

This piece poses four large questions:
* **Why pace?** Section 2 examines rationales for pacing and the complementary activities that give the temporary restraint of capabilities its value.
* **Pace what?** Section 3 considers where exactly one can intervene on AI progress, and the challenges in picking appropriately.
* **Pace how?** Section 4 follows the lifecycle of an intervention from anticipation to exit, asking what it takes for each step to succeed.
* **Then what?** Section 5 considers the broader effects of interventions, including on broader AI R&D, the economy, and the distribution of power.

By “pacing research” we mean technical and strategic research into questions like these. By the “pacing field” we mean a coherent field of pacing research gathering people with diverse views, producing results that actually build on each other. This field will span the AI evaluation world, the secure datacenter field, the AI economics field, and the AI policy field.

Despite the Pause movement and [years](https://leopoldaschenbrenner.github.io/xriskandgrowth/ExistentialRiskAndGrowth050.pdf) of preamble, pacing research is still mostly centred on politics rather than research. There is a host of research *bearing* on these questions: for instance the detailed [plans](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.04592) [for](https://ai-2040.com/) [specific](https://www.narrowpath.co/) [interventions](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05628), or the whole fields focusing on one component of pacing interventions (e.g. [model evaluations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.15324), forecasting, [compute monitoring](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.11341)). This paper seeks to organise this work into a field by offering a framework for unpacking pacing proposals and assessing them more rigorously. 
Pacing research should help us make wise choices. A shared understanding of the range of our options and the key dynamics at play would equip decision-makers to deal with the challenges ahead.
We use two stylised cases to demonstrate how our analysis applies to potential interventions throughout this document:
1 a coordinated cap on the compute used in frontier training runs, intended to address loss-of-control concerns, particularly the possibility that AI systems substantially accelerate their own R&D process. 
2 an international redline concerning models that materially uplift users’ ability to develop biological weapons, as an example of pacing diffusion (rather than pacing capability development).
